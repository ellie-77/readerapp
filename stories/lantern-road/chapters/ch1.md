The letter was under the third step, where the wood had gone soft with forty winters of rain. Mara only found it because she was looking for a coin she had dropped, and the coin had rolled into the gap and the gap had swallowed it.

She lay flat on the cold boards and reached in as far as her arm would go. Her fingers found paper before they found copper.

It was folded four times and tied with a strip of blue cloth. The cloth had once been part of something else. A sleeve, maybe. The ink on the outside had faded to the color of weak tea, but she could still read the name.

*For the one who keeps the light.*

* * *

Her grandmother had kept the light for thirty-one years. Then her mother, for nine. Now it was Mara, alone in the tower with the wind and the gulls and a lamp that needed trimming every four hours whether she was awake or not.

She did not open the letter that night. She put it on the shelf beside the tide tables and went back up the spiral stairs, and when the beam swung out over the black water she found herself counting the seconds between flashes as if the count might come out different.

It did not. It never did.

> "Some letters are written to be found," her grandmother used to say, "and some are written to be forgotten. The trouble is you cannot tell which from the outside."

In the morning, with the lamp cold and the kettle on, she untied the blue cloth.
